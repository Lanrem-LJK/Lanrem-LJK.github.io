Research context

Background and research objective

Metal ions contribute to enzyme catalysis, protein stability, and cellular regulation. Identifying which proteins bind metals, and how those metals relate to protein function, is therefore part of functional annotation. Experimental characterization and structural analysis provide detailed evidence, but require resources that make it difficult to characterize every newly identified protein.

Sequence-based prediction offers a way to screen proteins using their amino-acid sequences. This project addressed two related questions: whether a protein is metal-binding, and whether a metalloprotein belongs to an enzymatic or structural category. The aim was to develop classification workflows that could support metalloprotein annotation without requiring a structure as model input.

We first built a dataset linking protein sequences to metal-coordination and enzyme annotations, then constructed negative examples and trained three models using ProtBERT representations. Structural information supported dataset construction and sequence redesign; the resulting classifiers operate on sequences.

Methods · Dataset

Constructing the metalloprotein dataset

The dataset combined MESPEUS and MbPA to bring together their structural entries. PDB and chain identifiers were retained throughout processing, connecting each sequence to its metal sites and annotations.

1. Integrate structures and retrieve sequences

PDB identifiers from the two databases were merged and deduplicated. Metal, chain, and residue information was extracted from REMARK 620 coordination records. Biopython retrieved the deposited polymer sequences from SEQRES, including residues that were unresolved in the structure.

Sequences with at least 10% unknown X residues were excluded. Identical sequences were removed, and MMseqs2 clustering at a 90% identity setting reduced sequence redundancy.

2. Annotate and filter metal-site neighborhoods

ProDy extracted neighborhoods around each metal ion. A 2.88 Å cutoff defined close atom contacts, while a 5 Å radius defined the wider residue neighborhood used for redesign. Coordination records and neighborhood information were combined to check site completeness and chain consistency. Multichain sites were filtered, cofactor-associated sites were retained, and the final positive sequences were restricted to lengths of up to 2,048 amino acids.

These annotations describe both protein residues and nearby ligands, allowing direct and cofactor-associated metal environments to be represented in the same dataset.

Example: two neighborhoods around Mg in 13PK

In PDB 13PK, chain D, Mg422 lies 2.38 Å from Asp377 OD2. The nearest atom of Lys219 is 4.13 Å away, and Gly376 N is 4.74 Å away. Asp377 therefore enters the close-contact annotation, while all three residues belong to the wider redesign neighborhood.

3. Assign functional annotations

Enzyme annotations were merged from BRENDA and PDB, with BRENDA taking precedence. Chain-specific records were used when available, followed by entry-level annotations. For the classification dataset, sequences with an enzyme annotation were labelled enzymatic; those without an annotation in either source were assigned the category “Structural.”

Methods · Negative examples

Building two complementary negative sets

Training a metal-binding classifier also required sequences labelled as negative. We constructed two sets with different purposes: redesigned sequences close to the positives, and naturally occurring protein chains from the same structural complexes.

Synthetic negatives: redesigning the local sequence

ProteinMPNN redesigned residues within the 5 Å metal-site neighborhoods while keeping other positions fixed. This produced examples that remain close to their positive counterparts while altering the local sequence around the metal. Ten candidates were generated per sequence, using temperature 1.0 and negative biases for C, D, E, and H; the candidate with the largest redesign rate was retained.

Coordinate-based residue numbering was mapped back to the deposited sequence, accounting for numbering gaps, insertion codes, and unresolved residues. ESMFold and TM-align then screened candidates for structure-prediction confidence and similarity to the reference fold: mean pLDDT ≥60, RMSD <3 Å, and TM-score >0.5.

CHAI-1 provided five structure predictions per sequence for contact analysis. Sequences were retained when all five outputs showed either absent contacts or contacts shifted away from the reference positions used by the check. This yielded 9,666 computationally screened synthetic negatives.

Natural negatives: other chains in the complexes

Other protein chains without the selected metal-coordination annotation were extracted from the source PDB complexes. After removing duplicate and unsuitable sequence records, the set contained 9,661 natural negative sequences. These supplied naturally occurring examples for training and evaluation alongside the redesigned sequences.

Methods · Prediction models

Learning metal binding and functional categories

ProtBERT, using the ProtBERT-BFD checkpoint, converted amino-acid sequences into learned representations. We compared adapter fine-tuning, GANBERT semi-supervised training, and embedding-based SVM and XGBoost classifiers across three tasks.

For EoS, representation experiments compared pretrained and fine-tuned ProtBERT, CLS vectors and mean pooling, and multiple transformer layers. Class weights in SVM and XGBoost accounted for differences in class size. The ensemble combined GANBERT, SVM, and XGBoost scores in an MLP, with upsampling of the minority class during training.

GANBERT used additional unlabeled sequences in its adversarial objective: real sequences were encoded by the backbone, while a generator produced artificial embedding vectors from random noise. This provided a semi-supervised alternative to classifiers trained on fixed sequence embeddings.

Dataset partitioning used sequence clusters to reduce relatedness across splits. Each task had its own training and evaluation data, reflecting the labels and negative sources used by that model.

For ESNN's binary evaluation, the enzymatic and Structural predictions were grouped into the metal-binding class. The table reports each model's task-specific test population and F1 definition.

ESNN achieved 68.87% accuracy for the joint three-class task. Grouping its two metalloprotein classes gave 86.15% accuracy and 90.01% F1 for metal-binding classification. The EoS ensemble combined GANBERT, SVM, and XGBoost scores and reached 74.11% accuracy, compared with 73.2% for the best reported single XGBoost model.

The experiments also highlighted how the negative examples affect evaluation. MoN reached 76.59% accuracy with both negative sources and 78.61% on the subset containing positives and natural negatives. The two sets therefore provide distinct ways to examine performance on redesigned and naturally occurring sequences.

Project significance

A sequence-based workflow for metalloprotein annotation

The project brings structural evidence, sequence data, and enzyme annotations into a common dataset. Its curation workflow records metal-site neighborhoods and connects them to protein sequences, supporting both metal-binding prediction and functional classification.

The experiments show that protein language model representations contain information useful for these tasks. Adapter training, embedding-based classifiers, and score ensembling provide several ways to use that information, while the three-class model connects metal-binding and functional categories in one prediction workflow.

Because the classifiers operate on amino-acid sequences, they can support screening and prioritization when detailed structural information is unavailable. The dataset, synthetic-sequence workflow, and model code also provide a basis for further studies of how local sequence changes relate to metal binding.
