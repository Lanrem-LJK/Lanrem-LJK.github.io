Master's Thesis · Sequence-only Prediction

Advancing Metal-Binding Protein Prediction
with Deep Learning

A sequence-only framework that predicts, from amino-acid sequence alone, whether a protein binds a metal ion — and whether that metalloprotein is enzymatic or structural.

LAN Jingkai · University of Bern, Interfaculty Bioinformatics Unit · Supervisor: Prof. Thomas Lemmin  ·  Co-supervisor: Giulia Peteani
Overview

What this project did

A chain of decisions: each step exists because the step before it hit a real problem.

Metalloproteins drive catalysis, structure and regulation across biology, yet they remain heavily under-annotated in public databases because experimental validation is slow and costly. So we set out to build a predictor that works from sequence alone.

But good models need clean, representative data — and no existing metalloprotein dataset was large, broad, or clean enough. So we built our own: integrating two public databases (MESPEUS and MbPA) into a rigorously curated positive set of 18,340 sequences spanning 44 metal types.

Then came the harder question — what should a "negative" even look like? We designed two principled answer-sets (synthetic and natural), and finally trained three sequence-only models on a ProtBERT backbone. The result is a framework that predicts metal binding and enzymatic activity without any hand-crafted features.

Part 1 · Dataset construction

The data pipeline — each step fixes a real problem

Read top-to-bottom as a causal chain: a problem appears, and the next step resolves it.

Collect & merge PDB IDs

Problem No single metalloprotein database is complete — MESPEUS and MbPA each cover different entries and neither is exhaustive.

Solution Merge and deduplicate their PDB IDs, then download the corresponding structures. The merged list contains 81,256 IDs; 81,241 PDB files were retrieved. Keep PDB and chain identifiers throughout so that sequences can be traced back to their structural evidence.

Parse REMARK 620 for metal information

Problem MESPEUS/MbPA label metals from the HETATM region, so non-coordinating ligand ions (e.g. chlorophyll's Mg in 1c51) get wrongly tagged as "metal-binding".

Solution Re-derive chains, metal types, sites and residues from the PDB REMARK 620 coordination records — the coordination evidence used for this dataset. Non-protein chains are removed during sequence quality control.

Retrieve sequences

Problem ProDy's ATOM-based retrieval returned wrong or truncated sequences — DNA/RNA have no Cα atoms, and stray HETATM/metal atoms introduced spurious X residues.

Solution Switch to Biopython's SEQRES parsing, to recover the deposited polymer sequence, including residues missing from the resolved structure.

Quality control

Problem Sequences still carry unknown X residues, and some metal-atom names are non-standard (MOM→Mo, O1P→Pt, VG→V, WA/WB→W).

Solution Keep structurally meaningful X positions but drop sequences with >10% X, and normalize metal names.

Redundancy reduction

Problem 163,509 sequences are full of near-duplicates; in nature metal-binding regions are conserved, so near-identical sequences would leak between train and test and inflate performance.

Solution Deduplicate (→40,182), then cluster with MMseqs2 at 90% identity, keeping one representative per cluster (40,182 → 21,362). This 90% step reduces dataset redundancy; a separate 30% clustering step is used later to define model splits.

Extract & filter binding sites

Problem Some sites have no amino acid within 2.88 Å (multi-chain or external), and sequences longer than 2048 aa cannot be fed to the model whole.

Solution Use ProDy to extract 2.88 Å / 5 Å spheres, discard sites without usable within-chain coordination, and exclude sequences longer than 2048 aa. The 2.88 Å sphere identifies close coordination contacts; the 5 Å sphere defines the wider neighborhood used for redesign. Filtering leaves 35,339 sites across 18,340 protein sequences.

Annotate enzymes

Problem To train the enzymatic-vs-structural task we need a label, but BRENDA and the PDB enzyme database can disagree.

Solution Prefer BRENDA when available, fall back to PDB, otherwise label the sequence Structural for this dataset. This is an annotation-based category: a missing enzyme record does not experimentally establish a purely structural function.

35,339valid metal-binding sites

Part 2 · Negative sets

Designing the negatives — the hardest question

With a positive set in hand, we had to define what "not metal-binding" actually means.

Synthetic negative set

Problem We wanted negatives that differ from positives only at the binding site — forcing the model to learn metal binding itself, not other sequence features. But a naive redesign either fails to mutate the key residues, or distorts the whole fold.

Solution Redesign residues within 5 Å of the metal with ProteinMPNN while retaining the surrounding sequence, generate ten candidates per positive sequence at temperature 1.0, with biases −2/−1/−1/−1 for C/D/E/H, keep the candidate with the highest redesign rate, then filter in three stages:

  • ESMFold — keep structures with mean pLDDT ≥ 60.
  • TMalign — keep those still fold-similar (RMSD < 3.0, TM-score > 0.5).
  • CHAI-1 — keep only sequences whose metal ions no longer bind (distance > 2.88 Å). A sequence is retained only when all of its metal-binding sites pass this check.

Result: 9,666 synthetic negatives.

Natural negative set

Problem The synthetic set was too small to balance the positives, and its sequences were artificially close to positives — we also needed a realistic benchmark.

Solution From the same protein complexes, extract the non-metal-binding chains of heteromeric proteins as natural negatives, then clean duplicates, DNA/RNA and unknown-only sequences.

Result: 9,661 natural negatives — authentic proteins for realistic evaluation.

These are operational negatives based on the available complex structures. Their absence of observed coordination in those structures does not prove that they cannot bind metals under other conditions.

Part 3 · Models

Three models, three answers to "how do we learn this?"

Each model was shaped by the specific difficulty of its task.

The three tasks use related data but distinct training setups. MoN includes both negative sources; EoS uses only annotated metalloproteins; ESNN combines the positive set with natural negatives. The predictions are whole-protein labels rather than residue-level binding-site predictions.

Binary

MoN

Metalloprotein or not

Problem Synthetic negatives differ from positives by only a few residues, so the model risks learning trivial site-level differences and overfitting — and no near-identical pair may span train/test.

Solution Cluster positives + synthetic negatives at 30% identity and split at the cluster level (9:1), then train a ProtBERT adapter 5-fold and combine via majority vote.

Accuracy78.6%
F1 score82.2%

On natural (non-synthetic) test sequences.

Binary

EoS

Enzymatic or structural

Problem Enzymatic vs. structural is hard and imbalanced, with limited labels — yet we hold ~21k redundant (unlabeled) metalloprotein sequences that could still help.

Solution Compare three strategies and combine the best: GANBERT (feeds redundant sequences to the generator as unlabeled noise), embedding + SVM/XGBoost, and an MLP ensemble — with class weighting for imbalance.

Accuracy (ensemble)74.1%
F1 score (ensemble)72.9%

Ensemble beats the best single model (embedding + XGBoost, 73.2% / 72.8%).

Three-class

ESNN

Enzymatic / Structural / Natural-negative

Problem One model should classify all three categories at once, but adding the synthetic negatives would flood it with non-metal samples and skew the classes.

Solution Train on positive + natural negative only; mean-pool ProtBERT embeddings from layers 7 / 15 / 23 / 30 and fit a class-weighted XGBoost.

3-class accuracy / F168.9% / 69.2%
Metal-binding subtask86.2% / 90.0%

On the binary "metal-binding vs not" subtask, ESNN clearly outperforms MoN.

From sequence to a trained classifier

For the embedding models, amino-acid sequences are tokenized for ProtBERT. Hidden representations from a selected layer are reduced to one vector per protein using mean pooling or the CLS token. EoS compares both pooling methods and both pretrained and fine-tuned representations; ESNN compares mean-pooled features from layers 7, 15, 23 and 30.

EoS and ESNN use cluster-based training/validation/test partitions at an 8:1:1 ratio. Hyperparameters are compared on validation data, with class weights derived from training counts. Held-out test data are used for final evaluation. For the EoS ensemble, probabilities from GANBERT, SVM and XGBoost become inputs to a small MLP.

What we learned

  • ESNN > MoN — the three-class model beats the dedicated binary model on natural sequences, with gains of 11.5, 14.6 and 23.1 percentage points for Mg, Ca and Na on positive test sequences, respectively.
  • Embedding + ML > GANBERT — under the same split, embedding-based SVM/XGBoost consistently edged out the semi-supervised deep model.
  • Cofactor sites predict better — proteins with cofactor-coordinated metals (e.g. Fe in SF4 clusters) reached 94–96% accuracy; single-site proteins were hardest.
  • Synthetic negatives were too subtle — differing only at the binding site, the model struggled to separate them from positives, while natural negatives were easy.
